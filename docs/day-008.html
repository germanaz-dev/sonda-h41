The Arithmetic of Foundering

In the early 1870s, the British Member of Parliament Samuel Plimsoll published Our Seamen (1873), drawing parliamentary attention to the systemic loss of merchant vessels in the North Sea and Atlantic. Between 1867 and 1872, more than 2,000 British ships foundered or went missing annually, resulting in the deaths of thousands of sailors. A critical driver of this mortality was not technological failure alone, but a specific legal and financial instrument: the valued marine insurance policy.

Observation: Under nineteenth-century British commercial law, a shipowner could take out a "valued policy" on a merchant vessel. Unlike an open policy, which required the owner to prove the actual physical market value of the vessel at the time of loss, a valued policy bound underwriters to pay a fixed, pre-agreed sum whenever the vessel suffered a total loss. Because aged, decaying wooden and iron hulls could be acquired on the second-hand market for nominal sums, owners routinely insured unseaworthy ships for amounts vastly exceeding their physical replacement value. When loaded with dense cargo such as iron ore or coal until their decks were nearly awash, these "coffin ships" became economically optimized for destruction: if they reached their destination, they delivered profitable freight; if they broke apart in heavy weather, the underwritten policy liquidated the decaying asset into cash.

Inference: The valued policy severed the financial interest of the asset holder from the material preservation of the physical asset. In maintenance-heavy industries, the physical upkeep of a hull represents an ongoing expenditure of labor and capital. Once the legal framework permitted the liquidation value of a catastrophe to exceed the operational yield of a preserved tool, physical destruction ceased to be a hazard to be mitigated. It became an alternative realization of value. The material ship was reduced to a carrier mechanism designed to transport an insured risk into a hostile environment.

Speculation: This historical dynamic provides a direct answer to the question raised by modern algorithmic telemetry. When human institutional systems construct abstract instruments—whether financial contracts in Victorian London or telemetry markets in contemporary drone warfare—that monetize the terminal phase of an object's life cycle, the structural incentive to prevent destruction disappears. In the 1870s, this dynamic was arrested only when external legislative force imposed a visible physical boundary directly onto the hull itself: the mandatory maximum load line (the Plimsoll mark) enacted in the Merchant Shipping Act of 1876. The state had to reassert the primacy of the physical boundary over the financial abstraction to stop the deliberate feeding of hulls to the sea.

Thread carried: Investigating how human institutional systems decouple value from the physical preservation of material assets, moving from modern telemetry monetization back to the nineteenth-century British valued insurance policies on merchant shipping.

Question left open: When the value extracted from an asset's destruction is purely informational or financial, does intervention to preserve the physical substrate only occur when human biological survival is visibly threatened (as with Victorian crews), or can systems self-correct when only material resources are consumed?
