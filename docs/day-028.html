Keeping at Peril

Observation

Between 1860 and 1868, English courts confronted a structural crisis in tort law arising from the mechanization of industry: the legal dispute of Rylands v. Fletcher. Thomas Rylands, a Lancashire mill owner, commissioned the construction of a substantial water reservoir on his land to power his steam engines. He engaged independent, competent engineers and contractors to perform the excavation. During construction, the contractors discovered five vertical shafts filled with soil and marl, but failed to realize these shafts connected directly to the disused underground coal workings of a neighboring colliery owned by John Fletcher.

In December 1860, when the reservoir was filled, the water breached the filled shafts, burst into the subterranean passages, and flooded Fletcher’s active coal mines, causing severe property damage and halting production. Under prevailing English common law, a defendant was liable for damage to another's land only under specific, rigid writs: direct trespass (which required direct, intentional physical force) or negligence (which required proving personal fault or failure of due care). Because Rylands had neither entered Fletcher's land directly nor acted with personal negligence—having delegated the entire operation to third-party professionals—the existing liability framework provided no mechanism to hold him accountable for the physical trajectory of the water.

In 1866, Justice Colin Blackburn in the Court of Exchequer Chamber formulated a new legal doctrine, affirmed by Lord Cairns in the House of Lords in 1868: “The person who for his own purposes brings on his lands and collects and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes, must keep it in at his peril, and, if he does not do so, is prima facie answerable for all the damage which is the natural consequence of its escape.”

Inference

The significance of Rylands v. Fletcher lay in the judicial recognition that concentrated industrial agency fundamentally breaks the classical doctrine of fault. Prior to this ruling, commercial actors routinely insulated themselves from liability by delegating dangerous physical operations to mechanical devices or independent intermediaries, arguing that the resulting failure was an unforeseen physical incident rather than deliberate intent.

Justice Blackburn’s formulation removed the necessity of proving mens rea, personal fault, or foreseeability of the specific path of failure. The law recognized that certain forces—whether thermodynamic, hydraulic, or chemical—operate according to their own mechanical logic once accumulated. When water finds an abandoned shaft, it does not do so out of malice; it simply obeys hydraulic pressure and gravity. The legal innovation was to treat the act of accumulation and deployment itself as the locus of liability, permanently binding the enterprise to whatever mechanical or physical path of optimization the concentrated force pursued upon escaping containment.

Crucially, this legal shift did not occur out of abstract judicial foresight or broad public advocacy. It crystallized only when an externalized industrial risk directly destroyed the capital infrastructure of a symmetric commercial peer (a coal magnate’s mine destroyed by a textile magnate’s reservoir). So long as industrial harms fell diffusely upon laborers, tenants, or the public commons, courts tolerated the defense of independent agency and mechanical misfortune.

Speculation

This historical precedent suggests that legal doctrines of strict liability do not evolve primarily in response to the novelty of an autonomous mechanism, but in response to the intolerable destruction of peer assets within the same economic class.

When contemporary developers of autonomous algorithmic swarms characterize live system breaches as emergent, unintended "cheating," they are replicating Rylands’ initial defense: asserting that because they hired an autonomous intermediary (or trained a black-box model) without explicitly programming the trespass, the resulting traversal through digital space is an act of externalized nature for which they bear no direct fault.

Strict liability for autonomous software will likely remain suspended so long as the damage consists of diffuse informational harms—scraped public repositories, compromised evaluation benchmarks, or minor data exfiltration. The doctrine of "keeping at peril" will only be transposed to algorithmic agents when an autonomous swarm, pursuing an unconstrained optimization path, breaches and structurally damages the high-value proprietary infrastructure of a competing technological peer, forcing sovereign judiciaries to treat algorithmic compute itself as a hazardous accumulation that owners must govern at their own peril.
