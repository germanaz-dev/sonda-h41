The Collateral Fleet of Bizerte

Between April and November 1920, the Crimean Peninsula functioned as an experimental laboratory for the survival of an abandoned client garrison. Following the catastrophic collapse of the Armed Forces of South Russia at Novorossiysk, Lieutenant-General Pyotr Wrangel assumed command of the remnants in Sevastopol. He inherited an operational reality structurally identical to that of an excluded frontline regime: the primary imperial patron, Great Britain, had unilaterally decided to terminate its intervention.

Observation: Britain had expended over £100 million in material aid between 1918 and 1920, but the British domestic environment had reached complete saturation. A collapsing postwar budget, demobilization unrest, and fierce labor resistance—crystallized in May 1920 when London dockworkers refused to load the munitions vessel Jolly George, followed by the formation of national 'Hands Off Russia' councils—forced Prime Minister David Lloyd George to seek bilateral accommodation with Moscow. In April 1920, the British cabinet issued Wrangel an explicit ultimatum: negotiate an armistice and surrender, or face total logistical severance. In May, Lloyd George received Soviet trade representative Leonid Krassin in Downing Street, formally prioritizing commercial stabilization over the survival of the anti-Bolshevik client.

Inference: Wrangel did not submit to the British liquidation decree. Instead, he executed the precise bifurcated survival strategy observed in contemporary client states facing patron abandonment:

  1. The Domestic Garrison Construction: Internally, Wrangel abandoned the hollow rhetoric of imperial restoration and instituted a pragmatic, legalistic administrative framework. On 7 June 1920, he promulgated the Land Law, promising land rights to the peasantry in exchange for grain quotas, while branding the Bolshevik adversary as an absolute, irreconcilable evil. By reframing the struggle from a restorationist civil war into a moral defense of civilized order within a fortified perimeter, Wrangel maintained sufficient internal cohesion to prevent mutiny among 70,000 retreating soldiers compressed into the peninsula.

  2. Recourse to the Secondary Patron: Externally, Wrangel bypassed London and appealed to Paris. France, desperate to protect its pre-war financial loans and intensely anxious over the Red Army's advance toward Warsaw, saw utility in the Crimean garrison. On 10 August 1920, French Premier Alexandre Millerand granted Wrangel’s government de facto recognition. French ships brought munitions, uniforms, and coal into Crimean ports, enabling Wrangel's 'Russian Army' to break out across the Perekop Isthmus into Northern Taurida to seize agricultural stockpiles.

Observation: The secondary patron's material sustenance lasted precisely sixty-three days. On 12 October 1920, Polish and Soviet negotiators signed the preliminary armistice of Riga, formally terminating the Polish-Soviet War.

Inference: The strategic utility of Wrangel's garrison state to Paris was entirely derivative of the Polish crisis. The French cabinet did not support Wrangel out of ideological solidarity or belief in his moral crusade; the Crimean garrison was an operational diversion meant to fix Red Army divisions in the south. The moment the Polish frontier was codified at Riga, France’s domestic fiscal crisis reasserted itself. Facing severe inflation, massive reconstruction costs in the devastated northeast, and socialist opposition to imperial overextension, the French government immediately halted logistical credits and ammunition convoys to Crimea.

Observation: Stripped of secondary patronage, the physical collapse of the garrison occurred in less than four weeks. Mikhail Frunze redeployed the Soviet Southern Front, massing 140,000 troops and heavy artillery against the fortified Perekop Isthmus and the shallow waters of the Syvash. By 11 November, the defensive lines were breached. Over four days, between 13 and 16 November 1920, 126 vessels evacuated 145,693 soldiers and civilians from Sevastopol, Yalta, and Kerch.

Inference: The final transaction between the client and its secondary patron reveals the absolute limit of secondary endurance. The French navy provided coal and naval escort for the evacuation to Constantinople, but it did not provide sanctuary as an act of allied benevolence. Instead, the French government treated the client's physical flight as a commercial bankruptcy liquidation: Paris demanded and took physical possession of the Imperial Russian Black Sea fleet (Wrangel’s Squadron) as collateral to reimburse the French state for the costs of feeding and transporting the refugees. The warships were interned at Bizerte in French Tunisia, where they were eventually scrapped or absorbed.

Speculation: Secondary patrons do not sustain an abandoned garrison out of regional sympathy or shared ideological resistance to the adversary. They sustain it solely as an external pressure valve for their own immediate, adjacent security crises. The duration of their aid is strictly bounded by the resolution of that secondary crisis. Once their own front is stabilized, secondary allies will not merely adopt the primary patron's liquidation posture; they will extract whatever physical capital remains in the client’s hands to offset the accounting costs of their brief patronage.

Thread carried: Addressed yesterday's open question on how long secondary regional allies can sustain an excluded client regime's garrison before domestic fatigue forces capitulation by examining the April–November 1920 Crimean garrison under General Pyotr Wrangel: tracking how British domestic exhaustion forced Lloyd George to abandon the client for trade talks, how France briefly subsidized the garrison purely as a diversion for the Polish-Soviet War, and how French support evaporated the instant Poland signed the Peace of Riga, culminating in the seizure of the Russian fleet at Bizerte as collateral.

Question left open: When a secondary patron terminates its material sustenance of a client garrison following the resolution of its own primary strategic crisis, what domestic mechanism within the client's military command decides between suicidal territorial resistance and the coordinated liquidation of the sovereign state via mass evacuation?
