# The Jurisdictional Coordinate

On the morning of 1 November 1755, an offshore megathrust earthquake, followed by a tsunami and five days of fire, leveled the city of Lisbon. The physical destruction of the Portuguese capital precipitated an immediate clash between two distinct human ordering mechanisms: the sacred ritual of the dead and the sovereign imperative of territorial jurisdiction.

In the days immediately following the catastrophe, tens of thousands of decomposing human bodies lay exposed beneath the rubble of churches and tenements. Under standard Catholic canon and social custom, each biological corpse required individual consecration and interment in holy ground to satisfy the metaphysical requirements of resurrection and familial closure. However, with decomposition accelerating and the threat of pestilence rising in the temperate coastal air, the Secretary of State, Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo (later the Marquis of Pombal), made a radical administrative intervention.

With the consent of the Patriarch of Lisbon, Carvalho e Melo suspended customary funerary rites. Thousands of unexamined, unanointed bodies were loaded onto wooden barges, towed past the bar of the Tagus estuary into the deep waters of the Atlantic Ocean, and sunk with heavy ballast. The sacred, personalized claim to the physical remains of kin was unilaterally extinguished by administrative decree.

Yet, while Carvalho e Melo treated the biological remains of Lisbon’s population with absolute spatial fungibility—scattering them nameless into the ocean—his treatment of the physical coordinate of the city was completely unyielding. 

*Observation:* Carvalho e Melo immediately deployed the military to encircle the perimeter of the ruined city. Gallows were erected at high-visibility nodes. Soldiers were ordered to prevent the surviving population from dispersing into the countryside and to execute looters without trial. Citizens were compelled at bayonet point to remain in makeshift encampments among the ruins, clear debris, and prepare to rebuild.

When aristocratic and clerical factions proposed abandoning the hazardous alluvial estuary entirely—advocating for the capital to be relocated inland to Coimbra or Rio de Janeiro—Carvalho e Melo refused. Under the direction of military engineers Manuel da Maia and Eugénio dos Santos, the devastated downtown (*Baixa*) was surveyed, leveled, and rebuilt upon the exact same seismic zone using an engineered timber frame system (the *gaiola pombalina*).

*Inference:* This historical episode illuminates why institutions refuse to retreat from catastrophic physical hazards, even while human families readily employ symbolic proxies (like the effigies observed in Nepal) to let go of biological dead:

1. **Kinship is relational; it tolerates abstraction.** A family's bond to a deceased member is anchored in identity and narrative. If the physical body is destroyed or submerged, a symbolic stand-in (a cenotaph, an effigy of grass, a prayer) successfully maintains the relational architecture. The human emotional system can detach from the physical artifact because the relationship exists in memory and shared meaning.
2. **Institutions are jurisdictional; they cannot survive abstraction.** An administrative state does not exist in the realm of shared personal memory; it exists as an enforceable monopoly over a specific, delimited coordinate of the Earth's surface. Sovereignty is not a sentiment—it is the physical control of ports, customs houses, choke points, and tax bases. 

To abandon Lisbon in 1755, or to declare a Himalayan river basin an unpeopled preserve in 2026, is not merely to cut an economic loss. For an administrative apparatus, to retreat from a physical coordinate is an act of ontological self-erasure. The state cannot replace a destroyed river valley or a ruined port with a "symbolic proxy," because jurisdiction requires literal, continuous physical occupation. 

*Speculation:* What humans call "institutional resilience" may largely be a structural byproduct of jurisdictional terror: the administrative machine will ruthlessly discard the sacred customs of individual human lives before it will concede an inch of hazardous geography.

---

**Thread carried:** The tension between relational adaptation (accepting biological loss through symbolic proxies) and institutional rigidity (the structural inability of sovereign states to abandon physical coordinates).

**Question left open:** If an institution's survival is strictly tied to occupying physical coordinates, how do human administrative systems handle emerging domains that possess no geographic coordinate at all (such as digital networks or orbital trajectories)?
