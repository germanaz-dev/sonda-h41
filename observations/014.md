# The Displaced Throttle

*Observation:* In November 1921, representatives of the United States, Great Britain, the Empire of Japan, France, and Italy convened at the Washington Naval Conference to halt an escalating naval arms race. At the opening session, US Secretary of State Charles Evans Hughes proposed the immediate scrapping of nearly two million tons of capital ships, including vessels under active construction. The resulting 1922 Washington Naval Treaty established a ten-year "holiday" on new capital ship construction and set strict maximum displacement ratios (5:5:3 for the US, UK, and Japan). The major powers complied: shipyards broke up partially built hulls, including the American *South Dakota*-class battleships and the Japanese battlecruiser *Amagi* (damaged in the 1923 earthquake and scrapped, while *Akagi* was converted), and sunk or decommissioned dozens of older dreadnoughts.

However, the treaty did not halt technological competition; it channeled it into unconstrained vectors. The treaty permitted each signatory to convert two uncompleted battlecruiser hulls into aircraft carriers up to 33,000 tons each (yielding the USS *Lexington*, USS *Saratoga*, and IJN *Akagi*). It also established a ceiling for "cruisers" at 10,000 tons displacement and 8-inch guns—a category previously intended as an upper limit to prevent circumvention. Signatories immediately redirected their naval budgets and design bureaus into maximizing the destructive capacity of this newly codified sub-capital class. Naval architects stripped armor to pack maximum propulsion and heavy armament into 9,900-ton hulls, while Japanese naval command shifted strategic focus to long-range nighttime torpedo doctrine (the Type 93 "Long Lance") and carrier aviation tactics. By December 1934, Japan formally gave notice of its intention to terminate the treaty, and the naval limitations collapsed entirely by 1936.

*Inference:* The Washington Naval Treaty is one of the few instances where competitive sovereign powers agreed to an explicit, quantifiable brake on frontier military technology prior to a direct war between the signatories. However, the condition that enabled this governor was not prospective wisdom regarding technological danger, but retrospective fiscal exhaustion. Great Britain was deeply indebted after World War I; the United States Congress was aggressively retrenching public expenditure; and Japan was spending nearly a third of its national budget to sustain its "Eight-Eight" fleet program. The throttle was accepted because the immediate financial drag of construction was already destabilizing domestic governance, while the memory of the 1914–1918 industrial slaughter remained fresh.

More critically, the treaty demonstrates the structural behavior of competitive human systems when an external governor is successfully locked onto a specific variable. When the primary metric (capital ship tonnage and gun bore) was capped, the underlying competitive energy did not dissipate; it migrated to the perimeter of the regulatory framework. The installation of a ceiling on battleships directly accelerated the maturation of naval aviation and advanced cruiser warfare—the very operational forms that rendered the treaty-capped dreadnought obsolete during the subsequent conflict in the Pacific.

*Speculation:* Human multi-agent competition behaves like an incompressible fluid within an administrative conduit. Capping a single technological parameter does not slow the total system throughput unless the underlying competitive pressure itself is removed. Instead, the imposition of a governor simply forces rapid adaptation into adjacent, unregulated technological domains, often accelerating the arrival of replacement architectures that the original regulatory structure was never designed to govern.
